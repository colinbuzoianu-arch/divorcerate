Of all the ways couples hurt each other, one stands out in the research as uniquely destructive: contempt. In decades of studying how couples argue, the relationship researcher John Gottman found that contempt was the single strongest predictor of relationship breakdown — more than how often couples fought, more than what they fought about.

If that sounds dramatic, it's because contempt does something the other conflict habits don't: it attacks your partner's sense of being valued at all.

## What contempt looks like

Contempt is disrespect expressed from a position of superiority. It's not just anger — it's looking *down* on your partner. In everyday life it shows up as:

- Sarcasm and mockery ("Oh, *brilliant* idea.")
- Eye-rolling and scoffing
- Name-calling or belittling
- Mimicking or ridiculing your partner
- Hostile "humour" that has a real sting underneath

The common thread is a message, spoken or not: *I'm better than you, and you disgust me a little.* That's very different from a complaint, even a heated one. You can be furious with someone you still fundamentally respect. Contempt removes the respect.

## Why it's so damaging

Contempt corrodes the thing every relationship runs on: the sense that your partner is on your side. It makes repair harder, because it's hard to reach for someone who has just shown you disdain. And it tends to escalate — contempt invites defensiveness, defensiveness invites more contempt, and the relationship's climate slowly turns cold and hostile. (This is the territory of the [Hostile–Detached pattern](/patterns/hostile-detached), the one researchers treat most seriously.)

It's also often a symptom, not a cause. Contempt usually grows out of long-standing, unexpressed resentment — a pile of small hurts that never got repaired, slowly curdling into a low opinion of the other person. ## The antidote

Gottman frames the antidote to contempt as deliberately building a culture of **fondness and respect** — and, in the moment, describing your own feelings and needs instead of attacking your partner's character.

Practically:

1. **Catch it and name it.** Noticing "that was contempt, not a complaint" is half the battle. You can't change a habit you can't see.
2. **Swap the dig for the need.** Underneath contempt there's almost always a legitimate hurt or need. "I felt dismissed and it stung" opens a door; a sarcastic jab slams it.
3. **Rebuild the positives.** Contempt grows in a relationship starved of appreciation. Deliberately noticing and voicing what you value about your partner — small and specific — slowly changes the climate.
4. **Repair the backlog.** Because contempt usually sits on a pile of old, unrepaired hurts, genuinely working through some of those (often the point where a couples therapist helps most) drains the resentment feeding it.

## When to get help

If contempt has become the weather in your relationship rather than the occasional storm — if disdain, not warmth, is the default — that's worth taking seriously, and it's the kind of pattern a skilled couples therapist can help interrupt. Relationships do recover from it, especially when there's still fondness buried underneath, but it rarely turns around on its own.
