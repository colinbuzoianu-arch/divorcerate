You're in the middle of a difficult conversation and your partner goes quiet. No eye contact, one-word answers, or they simply walk away. To the person still talking, it can feel like being erased — like the other person doesn't care. But the truth of what's happening underneath is usually very different.

This is **stonewalling**: withdrawing from an interaction, shutting down, and stopping responding. It's one of the four conflict habits (the "[Four Horsemen](/four-horsemen-quiz)") that relationship research links to struggling relationships — but it's also the most misunderstood.

## What's actually happening

Stonewalling is usually a response to **flooding** — a state of physiological overwhelm where your heart rate climbs, stress hormones spike, and your capacity for rational thought and empathy drops. In that state, shutting down isn't a strategy to punish your partner; it's a nervous system hitting its limit and slamming the brakes.

Research has found that men stonewall somewhat more often than women on average, but anyone can do it. And the person stonewalling often looks calm or checked-out on the outside while being intensely activated on the inside. That mismatch is exactly why it's so easy to misread as "they don't care," when it's closer to "they can't cope right now."

## Why it damages the relationship

Whatever its cause, stonewalling reads to the other partner as abandonment. If one person is anxiously reaching for connection and the other keeps going silent, you get a painful loop: the more one pursues, the more the other withdraws, and the more the other withdraws, the harder the first pursues. That's the [pursuer–withdrawer pattern](/patterns/pursuer-withdrawer), and stonewalling is often the withdrawer's half of it.

Over time, repeated stonewalling erodes the sense of being a team. Problems never get resolved because the conversation can't stay open long enough to resolve them.

## How to change it

The antidote to stonewalling isn't to force yourself to stay in a conversation while flooded — that rarely works. It's to **signal and self-soothe**, then return.

1. **Name it instead of vanishing.** "I'm getting overwhelmed and I need 20 minutes — I'm not leaving this, I'll come back" changes everything for a partner who would otherwise feel abandoned.
2. **Actually self-soothe during the break.** Don't spend the 20 minutes rehearsing your argument; that keeps you flooded. Do something genuinely calming — walk, breathe, step outside.
3. **Come back.** The signal only works if you return and finish the conversation once you're calmer. A break that becomes a permanent exit is still stonewalling.
4. **If you're the one being stonewalled:** try to soften your approach. A gentler start-up and fewer [criticisms](/blog/contempt-in-relationships) make it much easier for a flooded partner to stay in the room. This isn't about it being your fault — it's about interrupting the loop from your side too.

## When to get help

Chronic stonewalling that neither of you can break is a classic reason couples seek therapy — and it responds well to it, especially approaches built around the pursue–withdraw cycle.
